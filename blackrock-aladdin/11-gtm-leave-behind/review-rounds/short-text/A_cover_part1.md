# Short-text readability, pages 1 to 9 (cover, executive summary, Part 1, Exhibits 1 to 9)

Test applied to every title, heading, column header, cell, tile, legend, list item and source note on these pages: read once at speed, do you know what it says? Items that pass are not listed. Superscripts are written as `^n^`, links as `[text]`, both kept exactly where the HTML has them. The Finding 1 headline the author cited sits on page 10, outside this range, so it is not covered here.

- **Current:** "Where the research points this role's first six months"
- **Trying to say:** what the research suggests the role should do in its first six months.
- **Rewrite:** "What the research suggests for this role's first six months"
- **Why:** "points this role's first six months" has no preposition, so the line reads as a verb missing its object. (Same text appears as the Part 3 title on page 15; change both.)

The title, subtitle, byline and disclaimer pass. The subtitle stacks three ideas, but they map one to one onto the three Part cards below it, so a skimming reader is not lost.

## Other notes on the navigation text (not readability, but what a skimmer meets)

- **"Three parts" versus "six pieces".** The executive summary says Studio has "three parts: APIs and events, a Python SDK and Aladdin Data Cloud on Snowflake"; page 3 says Woodhead's three parts are APIs and events, the Data Cloud and developer tools; Exhibit 1's Studio row lists four things (adds Aladdin Compute); Exhibit 4's title says six pieces. Each is defensible in context, but a reader moving by headings sees three, three, four, six. One sentence somewhere (Exhibit 4's intro, for example: "Woodhead's three parts break into six pieces in the public sources") would reconcile them.
- **Exhibits 3 and 4 have no intro sentence.** Every other exhibit in the range is introduced by a body sentence that names it; these two follow a source note directly. Items 21 and 26 suggest a line each.

## The five changes that matter most

1. **Exhibit 7 title (item 32).** At 25 words with a colon, a parenthetical and a "while", it is the hardest title in the range to read once, and it sits over the one exhibit a reader will quote. "Investment platforms, Studio among them, open to developers only after a relationship exists."
2. **Exhibit 1 title (item 11).** The first exhibit sets the pattern for all 18. "Of Aladdin's eight named products, Studio is the one for people who write code."
3. **Exhibit 4 title (item 26).** Three ideas in 24 words, and the second and third are already in the cells. "Six pieces make up Studio."
4. **Executive summary list items 5 and 6 (items 7 and 8).** These are the recommendations a skimming reader takes away, and both use shorthand the document has not yet introduced ("the Copilot plugin model", "what is in the box").
5. **Exhibits 2, 5, 6, 8 and 9 titles (items 15, 27, 29, 35, 40).** All five join two findings with "and", "but" or a semicolon; each has a body sentence or source note ready to take the second idea. Done together they make every Part 1 exhibit title a single clause of 15 words or fewer.
